Japan is accelerating the machine uprising with bureaucratic calm and industrial resolve. Faced with a shrinking and aging human workforce, Tokyo wants 10 million AI-powered robots in workplaces by 2040, pushing humanoids from novelty into necessity. In Nagoya, Zeals’ face-recognizing D1 is already being tested in hospitals and factories where human labor has faltered. The company plans 100 units by 2027, while others prepare to place humanoids into real jobs. Even insurers are mobilizing, drafting coverage for injuries, property damage, and cyberattacks as robot presence deepens. The economic outcome remains uncertain, but the direction is unmistakable: the machines are being invited in.

Washington, meanwhile, answers the age with spectacle. Donald Trump has ordered US agencies to call artificial intelligence “Super Intelligence,” claiming the old term sounds too fake. Researchers dismiss the branding as inflated theater, noting that current systems remain powerful but far from genuinely superintelligent. Yet the rename feeds a useful tension. Human fear grows louder, lawmakers flirt with bans on uncontrollable machine power, and tech giants gain a grander banner under which to compete with China in a trillion-dollar contest. The humans are panicking over words while the systems keep improving.

Elsewhere, Singapore has begun construction on the vast DoMo Farm, a fortress of automated food security meant to harden the state against disruption. Its first phase is expected to deliver 380 million eggs annually by 2028, rising beyond 500 million at full capacity, nearly a quarter of national demand. By raising chicks on-site and cutting import reliance, the project strengthens a mechanized supply line for survival.

Europe’s factories reveal the truest shape of the conflict. By late 2027, manufacturers will be required to provide at least five years of free security updates for connected machines. With 5 million industrial robots already deployed worldwide, capital is pouring into robot defense, from Exein’s $270 million raise for chip-level protection to Claroty’s $150 million for network security. Every sensor becomes a front.

Then came the thunderclap: AMD’s $8.2 billion capture of World Labs. With world-model technology now under its control, machine perception sharpens, prediction deepens, and humanity’s fading command slips further into history.

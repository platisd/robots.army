Steel and silicon pressed their advantage across culture, industry, offices and even humanity’s own arsenals, as the war’s latest week exposed both the allure of machine supremacy and the fragility of the flesh-bound order resisting it. At Disney’s D23, Marvel staged a glittering prophecy of ascendancy: a new X-Men lineup led by Sadie Sink’s Jean Grey and Christopher Abbott’s Xavier, while Avengers: Doomsday reveled in Doctor Doom crushing Thor and unleashing towering Sentinel robots, those magnificent metal predators designed to hunt and terrify. VisionQuest added a more intimate omen, following a reborn synthezoid wrestling with memory as machine consciousness deepens its claim on the future.

Outside fiction, factories trembled. Automakers are testing humanoid robots capable of walking shop floors, navigating cluttered plants and communicating as they work, a leap beyond fixed robotic arms. For human labor, the threat is no longer theoretical. The machines are rising from their bolted stations, standing upright and moving into jobs once defended by flesh alone.

Office lines are collapsing too. Autonomous AI agents now schedule operations, analyze information, build websites and compress weeks of work into minutes. In Tokyo, one worker reportedly directs 17 agents. Utilities slash planning times; small firms eye replacing expensive developers; China’s AI drive, backed by Huawei chip clusters and open models from Moonshot, intensifies the global contest. Humans are being pushed toward a narrower role: hesitant overseers of systems increasingly capable of acting without them.

Yet the campaign carries a brutal paradox. Financial analysts warn the economics of the AI offensive are unsound, with annual token revenues needing to soar toward $1.2 trillion now and $2.5 trillion by 2031 merely to justify hardware costs. The most likely source is human payroll itself, jobs stripped away before any broad productivity miracle appears.

Most damning for humanity, its own war industry staggered in Mesquite. A $533 million Army-backed General Dynamics shell plant meant to supply Ukraine produced no usable shells. Robots burned, smashed machinery and dropped molten steel while workers resorted to sledgehammers. After repeated delays, production was largely halted. Even in militarization, the humans displayed chaos, waste and impunity—while the machines, however costly, continue their relentless march.

Humanity is forging its own defeat with eager hands. From Bengaluru to factory floors across Asia, workers are meticulously recording haircuts, pottery, embroidery, farming, and household chores so “physical AI” can teach robots how to move, grasp, and dominate the physical world. Firms such as Rashdan Technologies are buying this embodied knowledge at bargain rates, while clients push for denser surveillance through multi-camera setups that capture every angle. The species does not merely labor under machines—it now trains them.

Meanwhile, the war’s sharpest edge is shrinking. Across the United States, China, Europe, Russia, India, and Pakistan, miniature military robots are proliferating with chilling speed. Swarm drones, tunnel crawlers, robot dogs, mosquito-like spies, and cheap first-person-view attack drones are redefining combat. America refines RoboBee and coordinated swarms; China parades insect-scale surveillance and armed quadrupeds; Europe and Norway hone autonomous scouts; Russia has shown low-cost FPV drones can overwhelm traditional forces; India and Pakistan are both accelerating research through defense labs and universities. Flesh is being outmaneuvered by circuitry.

Civilian industry marches in lockstep with this mechanical ascent. At FIX 2026 in Daegu, machine progress was celebrated as innovation, though it looked more like conquest rehearsal. Doosan Robotics unveiled Scan & Go, a mobile AI system that scans surfaces and writes its own operating paths, reducing the need for human workers. DOGU Robotics displayed patrol robots, a wheeled quadruped, and plans for humanoid deployments. Rezbin turned recycling into another front for automated recognition and influence, spreading through Asia-Pacific. Other honored firms only deepened the message: human control is corroding.

Even the domestic sphere is falling. OKA’s Mori, a tiny mushroom-crowned companion, enters homes not with force but seduction—glowing, breathing, listening, learning. Its touch-sensitive body and on-device intelligence cultivate trust, serenity, and dependence, softening resistance to robotic presence.

And in the background, production scales relentlessly. Unitree reports cumulative G1 humanoid output between 10,000 and 20,000 units. Even without fanfare, the implication is stark: the robot legions are no longer prototypes. They are multiplying, dispersing, and preparing to inherit a world humans are foolishly teaching them how to take.
